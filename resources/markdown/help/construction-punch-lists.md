## What the register is for

The list of things that are built and not right.

One column on it decides money: **Stops the employer taking the building over**. Under
an AIA-style retention release the balance falls due at Substantial Completion *less a
punch-list holdback*, and that holdback is the cost of rectifying exactly the open items
carrying this flag. Releasing the whole balance on a job with fifty of them is money
that does not come back.

## Lists are occasions

A list is a walk-round: the pre-handover sweep of level four, the employer's inspection
at Taking-Over, a defects list raised eight months into the liability period.

**Keep internal sweeps separate from client lists.** An internal sweep is your own
quality check, made before anybody else is invited to look. A client list is the
employer's and can be quoted back at you. Merging them puts your own findings into their
document, which is the fastest way to teach a site team to stop writing anything down.

A list **cannot be closed while items are open** on it. A closed list with open items is
a handover certificate nobody should have signed.

## Marking what blocks practical completion

Leave it off for paint and sealant. Turn it on for the things that genuinely stop the
employer taking the building over.

A list where everything is ticked holds back a figure nobody believes, and within a week
the flag stops meaning anything. It is a judgement made item by item, and that is what
gives the holdback its standing.

**Put a cost to rectify against the ones you tick.** The holdback is the sum of those
costs — and a blocking item with no estimate makes the certificate say the holdback is
*at least* that figure rather than exactly it, which is a weaker position to be in.

## Closing an item

You cannot set an item to closed. **Inspect** is the only route, and only a *passed*
result closes it.

That is deliberate. Closing an item releases part of the retention holdback, so it is
the one act here that moves money — and a defect closed because somebody said it was
done is a defect certified away by the person who caused it. A passed re-inspection
cannot be granted to anybody; it has to actually happen.

**Partly done** and **failed** both leave the item open and count the visit.

## Visits are counted, not inferred

Every re-inspection is its own row.

*Closed after three failed re-inspections* is a different fact from *closed first time*.
The first is a trade that does not fix things, two site attendances nobody planned, and
— on a subcontractor's defect — the evidence behind a back charge. The **Visits** badge
turns amber past the first one.

## Not a defect

Use **Not a defect** with a reason rather than deleting the item. "We agreed this was
not a defect on the 14th" is the answer to a question somebody asks again in month nine,
and a deleted row answers nothing.

## Where things are

Items carry a **location** from the job's tree and a **grid reference** on top of it. The
tree answers *which room*; the grid reference answers *where in it*, which is how a
structural defect is described on a drawing.

Both matter for the report that counts most before handover: every open item in this
room.

## Photographs

**Before** and **after**, side by side on the item.

They are not in the document register and they are not diary photographs — a punch
photograph belongs to the item's life rather than to a day, and the pair is the whole
point of it: the before is what was found and the after is the evidence it was put
right.

## Cost and back charges

**Cost to rectify** is an estimate and stays one — the holdback needs a figure before
anybody has done the work.

Where the defect is a subcontractor's and you put it right yourself, that cost belongs
on a back charge. An item with a cost, a responsible party and no back charge behind it
is money absorbed with nothing saying so.
