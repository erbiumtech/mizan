## An NCR proposes. It never deducts.

That is the most important thing about this register, and it is deliberate.

You can put a figure against a non-conformance and say it should be withheld. **Nothing
is withheld by doing that.** The figure joins a list that whoever certifies payment can
look at, and it appears on a certificate only when they choose it, confirm the amount and
their name goes against the row.

FIDIC 14.6 *permits* the Engineer to withhold; it does not require it. A deduction
appearing on a certificate that nobody decided on is the fastest available route to a
dispute — and it will be your dispute, because the client's copy has already left the
building.

## Disposition is the field that decides whether money changes hands

ISO 9001's control of nonconforming output, and it is a deliberate choice rather than
something the register guesses:

- **Rework to specification** — put it right.
- **Repair** — make it acceptable by other means.
- **Use as is** — the client accepts work that does not meet the specification.
- **Reject and replace** — take it out.
- **Concession requested** — ask the client to accept it, formally.

The last two lines of that list are where the money is. *Use as is* and *concession
requested* are the client giving something up, and that is normally paid for with a
reduction. The register flags one accepted with nothing proposed and no back charge
against it, because that is a concession given away for free.

**A concession needs its reference.** Asking the client to accept nonconforming work and
not recording what they said is how a job ends up with an as-built nobody can defend.

Dispositioning is a separate permission from raising, for the same reason: accepting work
below specification is not a call for whoever noticed the defect.

## Severity is not a proxy for cost

**Critical** means the nonconformity affects structural adequacy, safety or a statutory
requirement — the kind that stops a handover. **Major** is a real defect somebody has to
put right.

Do not set it from the money. A cheap structural defect looking minor is the one
direction that gets somebody hurt. The sidebar badge counts critical NCRs still open.

## The requirement, not just the description

"The cover is 22 mm" is the description. "BS EN 1992 clause 4.4.1, 40 mm minimum" is the
requirement breached.

An NCR that states only the first is an opinion. The second is what makes it arguable,
which is why the standard asks for both and why they are separate fields here.

## Corrective and preventive action are two different things

They are two sections on purpose. ISO 9001:2015 dropped preventive action as a clause and
every construction client's quality manual still demands both — and an NCR whose
preventive action restates the fix is exactly the failure keeping them apart avoids.

- **Corrective** is about this pour: what is being done to the work in front of you.
- **Preventive** is about the next forty: what changes so it does not happen again.

The register flags *fixed, not prevented* — the corrective action done and the preventive
action outstanding. That is the commonest CAPA failure there is.

Record **how the root cause was found** too. Five whys, fishbone, 8D. An audit asks, and
"we discussed it" is not a method.

## Closing needs a re-inspection

**Verify** points the NCR at a re-inspection that passed. Close-out then becomes evidence
rather than an assertion, and an assertion is what an audit finds.

Two things are refused: verifying against the inspection that *found* the nonconformity —
a closure proved by its own failure — and verifying against a re-inspection that did not
pass.

**Close** is refused without a verification. If it was never a nonconformity in the first
place, use **Not a nonconformity** with the reason: the number stays in the register,
because a gap in a register quoted by number is indistinguishable from a removal somebody
wanted.

## Taking a proposal up <!-- requires: ConstructionCertificateCertify -->

On the certificate's **Deductions** tab, *Offered non-conformances* lists what the quality
register has proposed for that job.

Choose one, confirm the amount — **withhold less if that is what was agreed**, which is
the ordinary outcome of a conversation about it — and the row goes on the certificate
marked as somebody's decision rather than as a figure the certificate worked out. The NCR
then records which certificate took the proposal up.

That column is written on the certification side, never here. It is the same shape as a
final settlement recording which payslip paid it: a proposal, not a posting.

Without the contracts module there are no certificates and the proposals simply sit on the
list. An NCR never deducted anything by itself, so nothing is lost.
